Problem solving still needs fundamentals

Why background knowledge and judgment matter when the problem itself is still unclear.

November 27, 2026

If someone asked me whether a campus shuttle had become more reliable, I’d probably start with how late it usually runs. Then I think about a rider who misses the same connection every Tuesday. The shuttle could improve its average by running closer to schedule at quieter times, and that rider would still spend Tuesday waiting for the next bus. I’d want to know what changed for that rider before I called the new schedule an improvement.

That’s the kind of problem solving I keep coming back to when I think about what students will do at work. I suspect more of the value will come from figuring out which question is worth answering. In this example, statistics helps me see what an average can hide. Knowing a little about how routes connect gives me an idea of where to look. Talking to riders might tell me why a delay that looks small on paper matters so much at one stop.

There’s also a kind of taste in deciding how far to go. If the missed connections mostly happen at one stop at the same time of day, a simple table might tell me enough. If the pattern changes from week to week, I’d want to look closer. That’s what makes the fundamentals interesting to me. The better I understand them, the more I can see in a problem that first looked like a calculation.

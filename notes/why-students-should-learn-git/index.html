What Git helps me remember

What I get from having a record of changes to code, writing, and shared projects.

November 11, 2026

I appreciate Git most when something was working yesterday and I can’t remember what I changed. I might have a guess, but looking at the last version that worked gives me something concrete to go on. I can see what changed and sometimes find a clue in a file I hadn’t even thought to check. Pro Git’s introduction to version control

I like having that option when I write, too. Say I cut a paragraph from a report and realize a week later that it had the clearest explanation. I can bring back the paragraph and keep the edits I made since. I also like finding a note about what I was thinking at the time. “Reorganize the results around the two main findings” might remind me why I moved things around. A name like report-final-v3 mostly tells me I thought I was finished.

The same thing comes up when I work with a teammate or an AI assistant. If an assistant edits several files in one pass, I can look at the diff, the comparison that shows what changed. Sometimes I find an edit I like alongside one I’d take back. Seeing them together helps me decide what I want to keep.

Even a Git repository that stays on my computer gives me that room to experiment. I get some of the same comfort from version history in a shared editor. I find it easier to try a different opening or rework a piece of code when I know I can come back to a version I trust. Git’s introductory tutorial
